# Power Rail Integrity

A synthetic four-layer board with one supply rail on it, set up for all four of the questions
railRF answers. Nothing here was measured on a real product: every shape was drawn to make a
particular answer legible, and the numbers below are what this workspace produces today.

Open **Sensor board ▸ Sensor board.crail**. The window opens on the Fast model with the board
already loaded; press **Run**.

## The board

30 × 20 mm, four copper layers, reference plane on **L2**. The outline is drawn on **Edge.Cuts**, so
the extent is on the picture rather than implied by where the copper stops.

| | |
|---|---|
| **TOP** (1 oz, 35 µm) | the regulator's output run, the ferrite, the run that lands at the loads, and every part's lands |
| **GND** (½ oz, 17.5 µm) | the reference plane, with an anti-pad round every barrel on the rail |
| **IN3** (½ oz) | two local `+3V3` pours — one under the load field, one at the regulator |
| **BOT** (1 oz) | the supply's long way round the connector cut-out, 0.20 mm wide |
| **F.Mask / F.SilkS** | every part's mask openings and its body outline, drawn by its own footprint |

One rail, `+3V3`. One source at the regulator's output pad: 3.3 V open circuit behind **40 mΩ and
50 nH**, which is the protection FET's on-resistance and the regulator's own output loop. Two loads
— `U1` drawing **350 mA**, and `U3` stating **no current at all**.

`U3` is the point of having two. A load row with no current is an **observation port**: it
contributes nothing to the DC solve and is still reported, and over frequency it is a place the
impedance is judged. The DC report lists it as *observed* rather than leaving it out, which is what
tells you the tool did not simply ignore a row you meant to fill in.

**The two IN3 pours are local, and neither bridges the board.** Each hangs off the rail at one
place and carries no through current. A single pour spanning both ends would put a second path in
parallel with the 0.20 mm BOT run and delete the finding Q0 exists for — which is worth saying out
loud, because on the picture the two arrangements look much the same.

## The parts are parts

Fourteen placements, and **every one of them is an instance of a footprint cell** — not a pair of
bare rectangles with a netlist claiming they belong together:

| refdes | part | footprint cell |
|---|---|---|
| `C1`–`C6`, `C11`–`C13` | 100 nF X7R | `footprints/C0402` (`smt:0402@N`) |
| `C7`, `C8` | 1 µF X5R | `footprints/C0603` (`smt:0603@N`) |
| `C9` | 10 µF X5R | `footprints/C0805` (`smt:0805@N`) |
| `C10` | 100 µF polymer bulk | `footprints/C7343-31` (`smt:7343-31@N`) |
| `FB1` | ferrite bead | `footprints/C0603` (`smt:0603@N`) |

Those four cells were **generated by circuitRF's own land-pattern generator** against this
workspace's technology, at IPC-7351B's nominal density, and committed. Nothing drew them by hand:
the lands, the mask openings and the body outlines all came out of the same generator the Footprint
picker offers, which is why they are on this technology's own mask and silkscreen layers rather
than on somebody's guess at a layer number. A test regenerates all four and compares them byte for
byte.

That is what makes the rest of this page readable. **Each part draws its reference designator on
Silk Top**, so you can see which pads are `C10` without counting rectangles; selecting a row in the
parts table highlights a part rather than an anonymous pair of pads; and a part can be taken off the
board — *Unmount* — without touching the artwork.

**`C10` is not shorted.** The question is worth asking, because a bulk capacitor whose return via
has no anti-pad under it shorts the rail to its reference and the board still solves. Here its power
via reaches the `+3V3` pour on IN3 and has an anti-pad cut in GND, and its return via reaches GND
and reaches neither IN3 nor BOT. The generator refuses to write the board if either is false, and
railRF reads a **566.5 pH** mounting loop for it off the geometry — which it could not do for a
shorted part.

`C10` is a **7343-31 (EIA D) moulded polymer** rather than an 0805, and that matters to the
mounting-loop story below: its two lands are 6.05 mm apart, so its loop is set by the barrels alone
with almost no mutual term left.

**This board is specified via-in-pad, filled and capped.** Seven of the parts have a barrel in each
of their two lands, and that is a fabrication requirement rather than a free choice: an *open* plated
via under a solder land wicks solder down the hole at reflow, and what comes out is a starved joint,
a voided one, or a tombstoned chip. Done properly the barrel is plugged — with non-conductive epoxy,
on this board — and plated over flat, which is IPC-4761's Type VII and is an extra step the fab
charges for. The plug is not a conductor, so the stackup's via entry is unchanged by it: a **0.3 mm
barrel with a 25 µm plated wall**, which is what every via figure below is computed from.

It is worth the money here for the reason Q2 is about, and the ranking is where you can see how
much: a via in the land is **555 pH** and 0.9 mm of fan-out to a shared via is **1201 pH** on the
same purchased part. If your board is not specified that way, the 0.35 mm fan-out row is the one to
read — those vias are outside the land and need no plug at all.

## Where the refdes come from

The `.crail` names three companion files beside the board, and they are what make a reference
designator mean anything:

| | |
|---|---|
| `layout/Board.clay` | the artwork — copper, barrels, anti-pads, and the fourteen placements |
| `layout/Board.ipc` | the board netlist: a net name per pad, and the refdes and pin it belongs to |
| `layout/Board.placement.csv` | the placement: a centroid and a side per refdes |

Without the netlist every port would have to be a **coordinate**, and railRF could compute no
mounting inductance for any part — a part's mounting loop is a property of *where it was placed*,
and nothing would have said where. With it, the ports read `U1.VDD` rather than a point in DBU, the
parts table fills in its **Position** column, and every mounting inductance below was read off the
artwork rather than typed.

`layout/Board.gen.py` is what writes all three. They are kept in step by a generator rather than by
hand because they have to agree: a pad in the netlist that is not under a land in the `.clay` is a
part railRF cannot locate, and a land with no anti-pad under it is a decoupling capacitor shorting
the rail to its reference. The generator refuses to write anything if either is true. It does not
compute a land pattern of its own — it *reads* the footprint cells, so the audit is run against the
same copper the board draws.

## Q0 — is it connected, and what does it cost

**48.6 mV** at `U1` against a **52 mV** budget. Met, and only just.

```
22.722 mV  46%   26.462 mm of 0.209 mm BOT copper       64.919 mOhm
14.000 mV  28%   the source's own series resistance     40.000 mOhm
 7.000 mV  14%   FB1's own DC resistance                20.000 mOhm
 2.367 mV   5%   2.578 mm of 0.468 mm TOP copper         6.764 mOhm
 0.637 mV   1%   9.334 mm of 0.227 mm BOT copper        22.264 mOhm
 0.536 mV   1%   1.183 mm of 0.841 mm TOP copper         1.533 mOhm
 0.536 mV   1%   1 mm of 0.324 mm TOP copper             1.668 mOhm
 0.512 mV   1%   the reference return                    1.464 mOhm
```

Those are the top eight of sixteen rows, and the window states the rest and their total. **Do not
add the millivolts up and expect 48.6** — the full sixteen come to 49.3 mV, and that is not a
disagreement. The table counts every group carrying current anywhere on the rail, and the port drops
only what is on the path from the source to it: where the rail divides into two legs that rejoin,
each leg drops its own millivolts between the same pair of nodes, and the port drops one of them
while the table lists both. The Breakdown card names its own total and says so on any board where
the two differ.

Half the budget is one trace. That is the finding this example exists for: on a compact board
with thin copper the artwork is not a rounding error on the parts, it is the largest single term
after the parts you already knew about. Widen the BOT run from 0.20 mm to 0.40 mm in the layout
editor, re-run, and the drop falls to **40.594 mV** — the copper term halves and the FET becomes the
thing worth arguing about.

Three via transitions, none over its current limit.

## The ferrite is a row of its own

`FB1` sits **in** the rail rather than across it: a part row marked *series*, with its two rail-side
terminals, a **20 mΩ** DC resistance and an **R-L of 0.05 Ω + 600 nH** over frequency. Which side of
it everything sits on is **measured off the artwork**, not typed — the copper is genuinely cut at
`FB1`'s two lands, so cutting the rail there separates it into two galvanically independent regions
and every row lands in one of them by where its own pads are. The run says so, and it names the
count: one source upstream, thirteen parts and two ports downstream.

At DC the element is a **ranked breakdown row** and not an annotation: 20 mΩ carrying the whole
350 mA is 7 mV, which is 14 % of the budget and the third-largest term on the board.

Over frequency it is what the rail's source branch IS above about 100 kHz — the regulator's own
50 nH is worth j31 mΩ at 100 kHz and the bead is worth j377 mΩ, and by 10 MHz the bead is two orders
above it. **What it does to the top of the band is nothing, and that is the point**: at 100 MHz
every capacitor is its own mounting inductance and the source branch is already irrelevant, so a
bead cannot fix the top of the band. It moves the low-frequency corner between the source and the
bulk, and it keeps the converter's own switching current out of the field — neither of which is a
thing the capacitor bank can do.

**Both observation ports are downstream of `FB1`, so they read the same curve**, and the run says
that rather than claiming otherwise. A port upstream of the bead would genuinely differ, by the
bead's own impedance at every frequency; the node up there carries no decoupling at all, so what it
would read is the regulator's output inductance — tens of ohms at the top of the band, against a
target written for a die. A monitor point judged against somebody else's requirement is not a
finding, which is why this board does not have one.

**An R-L standing in for a bead is optimistic and the result says so.** A bead's impedance is
strongly bias-dependent and its datasheet curve is measured at zero DC bias; at a few hundred
milliamps the same part is worth a fraction of its marked figure, and the curve looks entirely
ordinary. Attach the part's own measured impedance as a Touchstone file to replace the caveat.

## Q1 — does it meet its target

The band is 100 kHz to 100 MHz, the target a flat **55 mΩ**: a 13.75 mV ripple allowance against a
250 mA step. The rail **passes by 0.793 dB at its worst, at 100 MHz** — the top of the band, where
every capacitor is its own mounting inductance and nothing else.

Two anti-resonances are named rather than left to be found on the plot:

| | |
|---|---|
| 4.137 MHz, 17.868 mΩ | L(C9) against C(C7–C8) |
| 10.798 MHz, 19.79 mΩ | L(C7–C8) against C(C1–C13) |

and the converter's **fifth harmonic at 11 MHz sits 1.8 % away from the second of them**, which is
the coincidence check earning its place: neither the peak nor the harmonic is alarming on its own,
and 1.8 % is close enough that the two are the same event.

## Q2 — which capacitors are doing anything

The ranking re-solves the whole sweep once per part and reports what deleting it would cost:

```
C10  100 uF bulk   18.6 dB   -> -17.8 dB.  It is holding the low band up on its own.
C7   1 uF 0603      0.9 dB   ->  -0.1 dB.  Removing it fails the target.
C1   100 nF 0402    0.9 dB   ->  -0.1 dB.  Removing it fails the target.
C9   10 uF 0805     0.8 dB   ->   0.0 dB.  Removing it lands exactly on the target.
C4   100 nF 0402    0.7 dB   ->   0.1 dB.  Removing it still passes, barely.
C11  100 nF 0402    0.5 dB   ->   0.3 dB.  Removing it still passes.
```

**`C1`–`C3`, `C4`–`C6` and `C11`–`C13` are the same purchased part** — one 100 nF 0402 part number,
placed nine times — and they are worth three different amounts. The whole of the difference is how
each one reaches its vias, which railRF read off the artwork:

| | how it is mounted | computed mounting loop | worth |
|---|---|---|---|
| `C1`–`C3`, `C7`–`C10` | a via in each land — *filled and capped* | **555–566 pH** | 0.8–0.9 dB |
| `C4`–`C6` | 0.35 mm of fan-out | **708.8 pH** | 0.7 dB |
| `C11`–`C13` | 0.9 mm of 0.2 mm fan-out | **1201.1 pH** | 0.5 dB |

The first row is the one that costs money at the fab rather than on the bill of materials — see
*The parts are parts*, above. The other two need no plugged vias and are what a board built on the
default process gets.

Select a row in the parts table to see which one is which on the board — and, now that each
placement draws its own designator, to see it *named*. The number railRF prints for `C11` is

```
C11: 1201.1 pH — 552.1 pH power via + 16 pH return via − 2 × 2 pH mutual
     + 636.9 pH pad trace, with the pair 2.6 mm apart over 1065 µm of dielectric.
```

— and the **636.9 pH of pad trace** is the entire penalty. Two 0.9 mm stubs cost more than the
barrel they reach. That is a finding you can act on in an afternoon, and it is not visible in any
part's datasheet.

**Unmount `C10`** on the parts table and re-run: the worst margin goes to **−17.767 dB**, which is
what the ranking said it would. Nothing in the `.clay` is touched — depopulating a board is a
statement about what is fitted to the geometry, not a change to it, and the part keeps its position
and its computed mounting loop so it can be put back exactly as it was.

## Q3 — did the form factor break it

Press **Compare…** and pick a second `.crail`. The way to make one is to copy this workspace,
re-shape the board in the copy, and compare the two. There is deliberately no second board shipped
here: the comparison is about *your* re-layout, and a canned pair would teach the report's format
rather than the question.

The per-part table is the half worth looking at first, now that the mounting loops come off the
artwork: *a part that was 555 pH on the reference and is 1.2 nH on yours because its fan-out grew*
is the kind of row that explains a whole band.

## What the aggressors are

Three rows — `Y1` at 32.768 kHz, `U2` at 2.2 MHz, `Y2` at 26 MHz — and they are **typed, not
derived**. Each row is a name, a fundamental and a harmonic count, and every row says `Typed` on it
for exactly that reason: railRF cannot see what switches on your board, and a frequency somebody
pre-filled and nobody checked is the one that will be wrong.

They are an input, not a decoration. They put markers on the impedance plot and they drive the
coincidence check — which is where the 1.8 % above came from. `U2` is the regulator, and it is on
this board and in the netlist; `Y1` and `Y2` are a timekeeping crystal and a radio reference that
this synthetic board does not draw, because nothing about the check needs them drawn. To use them
on your own board, replace the frequencies with your converter's switching frequency and your
oscillators', and set the harmonic count to as far up as each one still has energy.

## Fast and Accurate — what this example is set up for

railRF opens on **Fast**, which is what makes the window re-solve while you edit. Fast reads each
trace section as one resistance from its own geometry; **Accuracy** meshes the copper instead.

Run both. On this board they agree closely:

| | Drop at U1 |
|---|---|
| Fast (the default) | 48.568 mV |
| Accuracy | 49.336 mV |

**1.6 % apart, and the Fast answer is the optimistic one** — which is the direction it is always
wrong in, and the reason the two are worth running once on any board you intend to trust. Most of
the difference is the reference return: Fast prices the plane at 1.464 mΩ from 38 coarse cells,
Accuracy at 3.636 mΩ from 364,829.

Both models report the same plane capacitance, **9.826 pF over 2.19 cm² at εr 4.3**. That is the
cheapest check on this page: one glance at it tests the permittivity, the overlap area and the
dielectric thickness at once, and a stackup copied from the last board shows up there immediately.

## What this example does not show

- **The mounting loop is a via pair and two pad traces, and nothing else.** It does not carry the
  part's own body inductance or the loop area in the plane of the board. On a board whose rail is a
  trace on the same layer as its capacitors — no power via at all — the computed figure is
  structurally low, and typing one is the right answer there.
- **Every observation port on this rail reads the same Z(f), because both of them are on one side
  of the ferrite.** That is a fact about where the ports were put rather than a limit of the model:
  put one on the other side of `FB1` and the two differ by that element's own impedance at every
  frequency, and the note saying they read one curve is then absent because it would be false.
- **A ferrite modelled as an R-L is optimistic, and railRF says so on the result.** See *The ferrite
  is a row of its own*, above.
- **Nothing above about 180 MHz.** The top of the band is a tenth of this plane pair's first cavity
  mode; past that the plane is distributed and the lumped answer is not the right one.
