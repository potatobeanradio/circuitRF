The Propagation Channel: a radio link in one schematic

Everything between a transmit antenna and a receive antenna that is not an antenna — path loss, shadowing, multipath, Doppler, rain and the atmosphere — as one component, so a transmitter, the path and a receiver are simulated together and read as received power, EVM, bit errors and outage.

The Propagation Channel (System, .cnl type PropagationChannel, prefix CH) carries what arrives at a transmit antenna's terminals to a receive antenna's terminals. The transmitter's amplifiers, the antennas or arrays, the receiver's noise and linearity are ordinary parts around it, simulated with it in one run, and what the run reports is what a system designer reads at the far end: received power, SINR, EVM after equalization, bit errors, outage. Its symbol and every parameter are on the Components page; this page says which model to pick and what each result means.

What it is, and what it is not

A channel predicts distributions, not a site. Every model here is statistical: a path loss and its spread for an environment, a delay profile and its fading for a kind of place. A run is one realization of that statistic, a drop, and the answer to "will this link work" is a percentage of drops, which a sweep over Drop gives. A particular street, building or hill is not in any of them. A ray tracer's output can be read in as a ray list (Multipath=rays) and a measured terrain profile as a CSV (P526, P1812), and nothing else about a site is: no map, terrain or clutter database is bundled.

It is one way. It carries the transmit end to the receive end and nothing back, so its S12 is 0. Power scattered back returns at the square of the path gain, −120 dB on a 60 dB path. A reverse link is a second channel; Reverse=CH1 makes it the same realization with its ends exchanged.

It adds no noise. A receive pin is thermal at the antenna temperature RxTant; a receive array's noise is its own, from what its elements' patterns see. The channel itself is noiseless.

What each analysis sees:

Analysis The channel
DC Open; the pins are their impedances
S-parameter, noise A two-port frozen at TimeOffset: S21 = H(f), S12 = 0
Harmonic balance The same frozen H at every tone, each at its own frequency, with no Doppler shift
Envelope Every ray delayed, faded and turned by its Doppler as the run steps

The bulk delay d/c (3.3 µs at 1 km, 3.6 ms to a satellite 1,075 km away) is reported as link:CH1:Delay0_s and never simulated: the envelope adds one step of latency plus each ray's delay in excess of the first.

Pin ends and array ends

A pin end is an antenna terminal: tx drives the channel, rx is driven by it, each over ground. Its antenna is TxGain/RxGain in dBi, the same in every direction, or one port of an EM result's far field (TxPattern="results/patch.cemr#1"), whose realized gain toward each ray and S11 are then the end's. TxAz, TxEl, TxRoll turn it; unstated, its boresight faces the other end.

An array end names a Phased Antenna Array (Tx=PA1, Rx=PA2), and that side has no pin: the array's own elements, patterns and positions are the end, and the symbol draws only the pins that remain. The coupling is two unknowns per ray, never a matrix of receive elements by transmit elements, and each receive element takes exactly the wave an incident plane wave from that direction gives it. Several channels may end on one receive array, their waves adding on its elements, which is how co-channel interference arrives with its own path, fading and direction. A pin receive end takes one channel.

The two mix: a base-station array to a one-antenna handset is Tx=PA1 with an rx pin.

The regime readouts

Whether a channel is "fast" or "slow", flat or frequency-selective, is a property of the run against the channel, and each run says which it is, in its notes and in explain --analysis:

Readout Cube What it means for the result
Maximum Doppler f_D link:CH1:fD_Hz Velocity·f/c, or fD, or the ends' relative speed
Coherence time T_c link:CH1:Tc_s Where the fading's correlation first falls to one half: 0.24/f_D for the classical spectrum. A record much shorter than T_c sees a frozen channel, and its statistics come only from many drops
f_D · T_record link:CH1:fDxTrecord Below about 0.05 the record is frozen; near 1 or above, the channel turns within it
f_D / subcarrier spacing link:CH1:fDoverSCS OFDM only: above about 0.01 the fading's inter-carrier interference shows in the EVM, and grows with it
RMS delay spread, coherence bandwidth link:CH1:RmsDelaySpread_s, CoherenceBandwidth_Hz A signal much narrower than the coherence bandwidth fades flat, all of it together; a wider one fades frequency by frequency, which an equalizer has to undo
Common Doppler ν₀ link:CH1:Doppler0_Hz A frequency offset the receiver must track; the demodulation measures it as CFO_Hz

A 100 kb/s FSK signal in an office (40 ns of delay spread, 11 MHz of coherence bandwidth) fades flat; a 50 MHz NR carrier on the same path does not. At 28 GHz and walking pace (f_D ≈ 78 Hz, T_c ≈ 3 ms) a 1 ms record is frozen; at 100 km/h it is not.

Fading and multipath

Multipath= says where the rays come from:

Multipath= Rays
none (default) The geometry's: the line of sight, and the ground ray for tworay
tdl with Profile= A tapped delay line: TR 38.901 TDL-A…TDL-E, TS 36.104 EPA, EVA, ETU, ITU-R M.1225 PedA, PedB, VehA, VehB, TR 38.811 NTN-TDL-A…D. The normalized ones need DelaySpread
file with File= Your power-delay profile: a CSV of delay (ns), power (dB), optionally phase, doppler, k and spectrum per tap
cdl with Profile= A clustered delay line, rays with directions and polarization: TR 38.901 CDL-A…CDL-E, TR 38.811 NTN-CDL-A…D. For arrays: beamforming gain depends on the angular spread, which a tapped line does not have
rays with File= Your ray list: delay, power, departure and arrival angles, cross-polar ratio or a 2×2 matrix

Each tap's gain fades by Fading=rayleigh, rician (KFactor) or nakagami (NakagamiM) with a Doppler= spectrum (classical, flat, gaussian, los), generated as a sum of Sinusoids sinusoids whose draws depend only on Seed, Drop and the channel's name. A clustered line's rays need no generator: each has its own Doppler from its directions and the ends' velocities, and the fading is the rays beating against each other.

Drops: a Monte Carlo over realizations

One run is one drop. A drop draws the shadowing (on by default, from the model's σ), the line-of-sight state when Los=auto, the rain's time percentage when Rain=drawn, the satellite state when States=p681, and every tap's or ray's fading. A Monte Carlo is a sweep over Drop, with no analysis of its own:

drop = 1
PropagationChannel:CH1 tx rx … Seed=1 Drop=drop
analysis MC type=parametric_sweep Var=drop Start=1 Stop=2000 Step=1 Inner=SP1
measure Prx_dBm = 10 + SP1.link("CH1", "Gain_dB")
measure Outage = outage_over(Prx_dBm, -98, "drop")

outage_over(x, threshold, "drop") is the fraction of drops below the threshold, with its exact 95 % interval as Outage:lo and Outage:hi; cdf, pctl_over, mean_over, std_over and yield_over read the same axis. Drop 37 alone equals drop 37 inside the sweep.

Cheap drops first. Received power over thousands of drops is an S-parameter sweep: each drop's link:CH1:Gain_dB is 20·log10|H| at the reference frequency with the antenna gains, shadowing and fading in it, and a drop costs a millisecond. The signal — EVM, bit errors — is an envelope run, seconds a drop, so it is read over tens of drops, or at the drops the power sweep picks out (the median and the one at the outage percentile, as the Chain Budget's Link bench does).

Reuse across drops. In an envelope sweep a transmit side that is the same in every drop is solved once and replayed into each later drop's receive side, which is exactly what solving both gives, because the channel is one way. A transmit array whose beam changes with the drop is solved once per distinct beam. The run says how many drops it replayed. A receive array whose channel cells are identical and linear is replaced by its feed for the run when the run has no noise; with Noise=yes the array is kept, because its cells' noise is part of it.

What drops cost. A receive array of nonlinear cells is stepped element by element, about 4 s a drop at 64 elements and a minute at 256 for a 1 µs record. A transmit array of nonlinear amplifiers costs the same per step, once per beam. So a link through large arrays keeps its receive cells linear, its beam the same across the drops, and its record a few symbols long; the examples below say what they traded.

The far end: equalizer, frequency offset and bit errors

Through a channel with delay spread, one gain over the record no longer reads a signal as a receiver would: a fade on some subcarriers reads as distortion. The envelope analysis's Equalizer= key says how each carrier is read:

Equalizer= Use it for
none (default) A circuit's own error, its ripple included, exactly as before any channel existed
genie The best a receiver can do: each subcarrier divided by the channel's own H(f, t), fading included. What remains is the circuit's distortion, the noise and the inter-carrier interference the fading causes
pilot What a receiver achieves: the response read from pilots (Pilots= on OFDM; a trained Taps= equalizer on DBPSK and single carrier), interpolated, with its estimation noise

The frequency offset (CFO_Hz, CFOdrift_Hzps) is measured in every mode and removed before genie and pilot. A single carrier or DBPSK carrier is first acquired to the nearest bin of the window from its power spectrum, so a satellite's hundreds of kilohertz of Doppler are read to a fraction of a hertz, then fitted from the symbols' phases. A drift is measurable only when it moves the frequency within the record: a low-orbit pass's kilohertz a second moves a 0.2 ms record by a fifth of a hertz, so the channel's own drift, link:CH1:Drift0_Hzps, is the one to read.

Errors. BER, SER, BitErrors, Bits and the exact interval BERlo/BERhi are counted after the decisions; BERest estimates the BER from each symbol's SINR, treating noise and distortion as Gaussian, and reaches below what a short record can count. SINR_dB per symbol maps to a coded error rate of your own; forward error correction is not modelled. The cubes and their definitions: Simulations › Equalizer, frequency offset and bit errors.

Picking the beam

When an end is a Feed-mode array whose Phase is a steer(), the run picks its beam through the drop's channel. Each end's candidates are a grid in (u, v) one beamwidth apart out to ScanLimit, each made through the array's own steer() and PhaseBits (or Codebook= names a CSV of directions). Every pair is scored by the power the two array factors carry through the drop's rays, averaged over the signal's band, through the linear pattern model; the run that follows is the full circuit.

BeamSelect= Beam used
auto (default) The strongest pair
rank with BeamRank=n The n-th strongest distinct pair: one within a beamwidth of a stronger pair at both ends is the same path, so rank 2 is the next path, not the neighbouring beam
index with TxBeam=, RxBeam= A codebook entry (a number from 1) or a direction (θ, φ) per end
manual The arrays' own weights, as written

The beam is set for the run only; the schematic keeps its steer(). The run reports every pair scored (link:CH1:Beam_dB[pair], strongest first), the pair chosen, and BeamBound_dB, the best any phase-only weights could do through these rays, so BeamCodebookLoss_dB is the price of the codebook.

Picking another beam in the tool. In the Phased Array tool (Tools ▸ Phased Array), an array that is a channel end has a Channel tab: the drop's rays on the (u, v) map — departures on a transmit array, arrivals on a receive array, sized by power — with the candidates, and the ranked pairs in a list beside it. Click a pair or a ray to use it: that writes BeamSelect=index with TxBeam/RxBeam on the channel. The check button (Apply to array) writes the directions into the arrays' steer() and sets BeamSelect=manual. Each is one undo step. The drop slider shows another realization without changing anything.

Satellite links

PathLoss=satellite takes the orbit's Altitude and the Elevation the earth station sees it at, and gives the slant range, the latency, and the Doppler and its rate for a rising or setting Pass at the frequency. A low orbit at 600 km seen at 30° is 1,075 km away (3.6 ms) and moves the carrier by about 400 kHz at 20 GHz. Scenario= adds TR 38.811's line-of-sight probability, shadowing and clutter for a terminal in dense urban, urban, suburban or rural surroundings.

The atmosphere is taken at Availability: Rain=fixed adds P.618's attenuation exceeded for 100 − Availability % of the time, the budget's number; Rain=drawn draws the time percentage per drop, so a drop sweep's rain statistics are the model's. Gas, Clouds, Scintillation and Ionosphere add their terms the same way.

States=p681 with StateEnvironment= applies ITU-R P.681's land mobile-satellite model: the link moves between a GOOD state (line of sight, light shadowing) and a BAD state (heavy shadowing and blockage), each with its own direct-signal and multipath statistics, valid from 1.5 to 20 GHz above 20° elevation. A terminal moving at Velocity crosses states along the record; a record much shorter than a state is one state, so the fraction of BAD drops is the fraction of time the link is shadowed. The run reports link:CH1:State (1 GOOD, 0 BAD).

A transponder is ordinary parts: an input filter, an amplifier at its back-off, an output filter, and the channel after it. DopplerComp=common pre-compensates the common Doppler at the transmit end, as a terminal that knows the ephemeris does.

The far-field warning

The channel takes each end to be in the other's far field: its rays are plane waves. check and the run warn when the ends are closer than 2D²/λ for an array's largest dimension D, its aperture's diagonal. A 16 × 16 half-wavelength array at 28 GHz is 121 mm corner to corner and needs 2.7 m; a 64 × 64 one needs 44 m, so a 10 m indoor link to it is in its near field and the gain the channel gives is overstated. Near-field coupling between ends is not modelled.

Examples

Three examples build a link each (File ▸ New ▸ Examples):

  • Indoor IoT Link — an 868 MHz FSK sensor two floors from its gateway: P.1238 with partitions, a tapped delay line read from a power-delay profile file, received power and outage over 2,000 drops, and bit errors over 50.
