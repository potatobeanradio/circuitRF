How results are stored

Every analysis returns a DataSet — a named bundle of labelled, unit-bearing arrays (DataCubes), each a single kind, real or complex. A whole run's DataSets go into one file in the workspace's shared results/ folder, and you name that file in the Results file field above the analyses list; leaving it blank uses the schematic's own name. This is what the Data Display plots and what .npy / .mat export produces. Full detail: Where results live.

A new analysis shows its frequencies in the preferred unit from Settings ▸ General ▸ Units; a unit the analysis or the schematic already states wins.

DC (operating point)

Computes: the steady-state node voltages and branch currents with no signal applied — the bias point. For nonlinear circuits this is the prerequisite to harmonic balance.

Method: modified nodal analysis (MNA); nonlinear devices solved by Newton–Raphson with gmin and source stepping for convergence robustness.

SettingMeaning
TolNewton convergence tolerance on the residual.
MaxIterMaximum Newton iterations.
gmin / source steppingContinuation aids that ease a hard bias point into convergence.

Read currents directly with an IProbe in series, and name a voltage with a VProbe on the wire.

S-Parameters

Computes: the linear, small-signal multiport network parameters over a frequency sweep — the S-matrix at each frequency, with renormalization to the port reference impedances. Ports are defined by Term components (or P1Tone port numbers).

Method: complex MNA solved per frequency point; each port is excited in turn and the scattered waves are extracted and renormalized. Embedded Touchstone (SnP) blocks are interpolated onto the sweep.

For the ideal System Components this is the analysis that answers almost everything about them: port match, isolation, coupling and directivity, a filter's passband and rejection, a duplexer's arm-to-arm leakage, and an amplifier's small-signal gain and stability.

From the CLI: circuitrf sparam circuit.cnl --freq 1GHz:10GHz:50MHz -o out.s2p.

Noise: which analysis answers what

Noise is not an analysis of its own. Five analyses compute it, each switched on with Noise=yes (on the oscillator, PhaseNoise=yes), and each answers a different question. Without the key, every one of them runs exactly as before.

  • S-parameters: the small-signal noise figure of a two-port at its DC bias. NF and the four noise parameters (NFmin, Sopt, Rn), the noise temperatures, each part's share of the noise, and for any number of ports the correlation of the outgoing noise waves. This is where an LNA is designed, with the noise, gain and stability circles.
  • Harmonic balance: the noise figure with a large signal in the circuit. A mixer's SSB and DSB noise figure with its LO running, an LNA's noise figure while a blocker drives it, and how much noise folds in through each sideband.
  • Loadpull and sourcepull: NF as one more metric at every termination and drive level, with contours, for the trade between noise, gain and output power.
  • Envelope: thermal noise and a source's phase-noise mask in a modulated run, and what they do to the signal: EVM, SNR and the constellation, split into the noise's part and the distortion's.
  • Oscillator: an oscillator's phase noise from the circuit's own noise sources, which an envelope run's LO can then carry.

Each part's noise model is the same in all five. Passive parts are thermal at their own temperature. An SnP block with Touchstone noise data is noisy by that data. Diodes, BJTs, MOSFETs and FETs have shot, channel, thermal and flicker noise as their models declare. A system Amplifier and Mixer are noisy by their NF, and a compiled model by the sources it declares. A part the analysis cannot give noise to is named in a warning, and Noise=no on a part switches its noise off without one. circuitrf explain <file> --analysis lists every part's noise model.

Conventions
Densities Single-sided and absolute. A matched load's outgoing noise wave is kT W/Hz, a resistor's open-circuit noise voltage 4kTR V²/Hz, with k = 1.380649e-23 J/K exactly. Phase noise L(f) is single-sided, in dBc/Hz.
Reference temperature A noise figure is referred to T0 = 290 K: its input termination is at T0.
Part temperature Each part is at its own physical temperature: its Temp, or Dtemp above the ambient, or the ambient. The default ambient is 26.85 °C, which is 300 K, not T0. A matched 6 dB pad at 300 K reads 6.111 dB, not 6.000 dB. temp = 16.85 puts the whole design at 290 K.
Noise data Noise data from a file is absolute, at T0, and does not move with the ambient. A device's Tg and Td are noise temperatures in kelvin.
Ports and sources Noiseless. A port's termination enters only as the T0 reference of a noise figure. In an envelope run, Noise=yes on a source makes its impedance thermal.
Random noise Only an envelope run draws random noise, from Seed (1 by default). The same seed gives the same bits on every computer and in every split of the run.

Small-signal noise

Noise=yes adds the circuit's small-signal noise to the S-parameter run. The noise comes from the same solve: one more back-substitution per port at each frequency, and no further factorization. S does not change. Noise=no, the default, adds nothing.

analysis SP1 type=sparam start=1 stop=6 npts=51 Unit=GHz Noise=yes

What the run adds. The cubes go into the analysis's own group, beside S and Z0. A parametric sweep stacks them as it stacks S.

Every cube but Cs and NCs is for a two-port only, because a noise figure and noise parameters belong to a two-port. The names are the ones commonly used for them. The linear noise factor is Fn rather than F, because SP1.F("probe") already reads a WSProbe's return difference.

A network with more than two ports gets Cs only. For the noise figure from port 1 to any other port, or from any other source, and for constant-noise-figure circles, use nf(), te(), rn() and ns_circle(): nf(SP1.S, 0, 3) is the noise figure from port 1 to port 3 of a three-port. Gain and stability circles to draw beside the noise circles are ga_circle(), gp_circle() and stab_circle(), and the Noise and gain circles chart shows all of them in one action.

What is noisy.

  • Every linear passive part is thermal at its own temperature. That is its Temp, or Dtemp above the ambient, or else the design's ambient. The noise is computed from the part's own small-signal stamp (Bosma's theorem), so a lossless part adds none, and a lossy one adds exactly the noise of its loss. Resistors, lossy reactances, transmission lines and coupled inductors (Mutual) are all covered.
  • An SnP block whose two-port file has a Touchstone noise block is noisy by that data, even if it has gain. A passive file with no noise block is thermal, like any passive part.
  • The built-in nonlinear models are noisy at their DC operating point: a diode's and a BJT's shot and flicker noise and their series resistances, a MOSFET's channel noise, and a FET's Ri, Tg, Td and Kf (Components). A FET with none of those keys set has no noise sources.
  • A system Amplifier is noisy by its NF (and NFopt, Gopt, Rn). The other system blocks are thermal by their loss.
  • A compiled model is noisy by the sources it declares (white_noise, flicker_noise). They are evaluated at the DC operating point the run linearises the device about, and multiplied by the instance's multiplicity.
  • A Z_Port whose impedance has a real part that is positive semidefinite at every swept frequency is passive, and thermal like any passive part. One with a negative real part anywhere is noiseless.
  • An SnP block's temperature is its own Temp or Dtemp, as on any part. It matters only for a passive block with no noise data, whose noise is thermal. Noise data from a file is absolute and does not change with it.

What is noiseless. The following parts have no noise model here:

  • an SDD, and a FET with no noise keys;
  • active or black-box linear parts, such as a controlled source, an amplifier block with no NF, or a negative resistor;
  • a Mixer, which has no conversion path in an S-parameter run;
  • an SnP block with gain and no noise data;
  • every Deembed;
  • a compiled model that declares no noise sources.

These are taken as noiseless, and one warning names them all (sparam.noise.noiseless), so a result never leaves out a part without saying so. Ports and independent sources are noiseless by definition, and they are not named.

Switching a part's noise off. Noise=no on a part makes it noiseless in this analysis:

R:Rbias  b 0  R=10 kOhm Noise=no

Use it for a resistor that stands in for an instrument's load, or one that is part of a model and not a physical resistor. The part is not named in the warning, because the statement is yours. Noise=yes, the default, leaves the part as noisy as its model. The key is accepted on every part: on a Mutual or any of the inductors it couples it quiets the whole coupled group, and on a port, a source or a probe it does nothing (check says so, as check.noise.inert-switch). In the parameter editor it is the Noise check box in the part's own list. Its show-on-schematic box draws it on the symbol, and is off by default.

Which part sets the noise

A two-port run also writes NFc and NFpct on a part axis. The axis has one entry per noisy part, labelled with its instance path. A Mutual and the inductors it couples are one entry, labelled with their names joined by +, because their loss is one network. NFc is the part's own output noise at port 2 divided by the source's, with port 1 driven from its Z0, so F − 1 is their sum. NFpct is each one as a percentage of F − 1. The shares come from the same solve as Cs, at no extra cost. To see them, plot NFpct against part at one frequency on a rectangular plot.

NoiseContrib=yes also writes NCs, each part's share of the whole Cs matrix, for any number of ports. Its size is frequencies × parts × ports² complex values, which is why it is not written by default. circuitrf explain bench.cnl --analysis states that size, and lists every part's noise model: thermal at its temperature in K, the file and band of an SnP block's noise data, a compiled model's noise sources, switched off, or noiseless with the reason. Read it before trusting an NF.

Conventions and noise data

The densities, T0 and the ambient are in Noise ▸ Conventions. Two more belong to this analysis:

  • The noise waves are the power waves S is defined on. Every port is terminated in its own noiseless Z0. This is why a port whose Z0 has no positive real part is refused with Noise=yes (sparam.noise.reactive-z0): there is no termination to define the waves against.
  • Sopt and Zopt are on Re Z0 of port 1, and Rn is in ohms. A Touchstone row's rn is Rn over that R, which rn(SP1.S) gives.

An SnP block's noise data. circuitRF reads Touchstone 1.x noise rows: frequency, NFmin in dB, |Γopt|, ∠Γopt in degrees, and rn normalised to the option line's R. Between rows the noise parameters are linear. Outside the rows the nearest row holds, and sparam.noise.data-band says so. A row that no physical two-port could have (0 ≤ Fmin − 1 ≤ 4·Rn·Re Yopt fails) gets sparam.noise.data-unphysical, and it is used as given.

Written out. circuitrf sparam bench.cnl -o out.s2p on a two-port writes a Touchstone noise block after the data. It is in the same 1.x format, with Γopt and rn on the option line's R. The rows are the run's own cubes, so reading the file back gives them again.

Harmonic Balance

Computes: the steady-state response of a nonlinear circuit driven by one or more tones (up to six) — the spectrum (DC + harmonics, and mixing products when there is more than one tone) at every node. From it come Pout, gain, efficiency, PAE, compression, and intermodulation.

Method: the circuit is split into a linear sub-network (solved in the frequency domain) and nonlinear devices (evaluated in the time domain and transformed by FFT). A multidimensional Newton solve drives the harmonic-balance residual — frequency-domain KCL — to zero, using a conversion-matrix Jacobian. Power/source continuation ramps the drive to reach convergence deep into compression. Multi-tone analysis uses a diamond-truncated mixing spectrum.

For the ideal System Components this is the analysis for everything an S-parameter run cannot reach — a mixer's conversion gain and its mixing products, an amplifier's compression and IM3, and passive intermodulation on the blocks that carry it. Two tones (multi-tone) are what an intercept measurement needs.

Noise in harmonic balance

Computes: the noise figure of a circuit with a large signal in it — a mixer's SSB and DSB noise figure, an LNA's noise figure while a blocker drives it, or a receiver chain's with its LO running. The S-parameter noise analysis cannot answer these: it linearises at the DC operating point, so it has no LO, no blocker and no frequency conversion.

analysis HB1 type=hb Tone=LOfreq MaxHarm=7 SSStart=10 SSStop=200 SSNpts=20 SSUnit=MHz Noise=yes NoiseIn=1 NoiseOut=2 NoiseSideband=1

The small-signal frequencies are the output frequencies — for a mixer, the IF. NoiseSideband says where the signal comes in: the input frequency is f_out + k·f0.

A down-converter with f_IF = f_LO − f_RF -1
Two tones (a blocker and an LO) one integer per tone, 1,0

What is noisy.

  • Passive parts are thermal at their own temperature, at every sideband frequency.
  • A nonlinear device's noise sources are read at every time sample of its large-signal waveform, so its noise is correlated between sidebands: a diode switched by the LO is noisy only while it conducts. A flicker term is taken at the geometric mean of the two sidebands' frequencies.
  • A Mixer's NF is an equivalent noise source at its RF port.
  • The input port's termination is at T0 at every sideband; the other ports are noiseless, as in the S-parameter noise analysis.

A device's noise is correlated between sidebands, so it cannot be split by sideband. NFsb shows the noise from the terminations and passive parts, and it adds up to 100 % less the devices' share. Each device's share is in NFc.

A compiled (Verilog-A) device reports its noise for the whole waveform in one request per operating point.

Inside a parametric sweep, LO power or blocker power is an ordinary sweep axis, so "NF against LO drive" and "NF against blocker level" need nothing more. With the drive turned down to nothing, NF equals the S-parameter noise analysis's.

Oscillator

An oscillator analysis finds a free-running oscillation: its frequency, its amplitude, its harmonics, and how they tune. Harmonic balance alone cannot do this. It needs the fundamental stated, and an oscillator's frequency is part of the answer. Started on an oscillator, it finds the circuit sitting still at its DC operating point, which is always a solution.

Place one OscProbe in the oscillator's loop (series) or across its resonator (shunt), and add an analysis of type Oscillator:

OscProbe:OP1  c 0  Mode=shunt
analysis OSC1 type=osc Fguess=140 Unit=MHz MaxHarm=9

How it works. At the OscProbe the analysis inserts a generator that works at one frequency f, with amplitude A. For any trial (A, f) an ordinary harmonic-balance run at f gives the current the generator delivers (shunt) or the voltage across it (series). Where that is zero, the generator is doing no work and the circuit sustains itself. That is two equations, the real and imaginary parts, in two unknowns, solved by Newton's method around harmonic-balance runs.

Start-up. First, the circuit is linearised about its DC operating point, and the admittance looking into the probe is computed from Fstart to Fstop (by default half and one and a half times Fguess). The circuit starts to oscillate where that admittance has a negative real part at a zero of its imaginary part: the circuit gives back more power than it takes, at a frequency where it resonates. If no such point is found, the run stops and says so, naming the span it searched. It never returns the DC solution as an oscillation. Then the amplitude is raised from small until the negative resistance runs out, which brackets the oscillation, and Newton's method finishes it.

Results. The run writes the cubes a harmonic-balance analysis writes, at the oscillation (V, I, ToneFreqs and the rest), so every HB measurement and Data Display preset works on it. It adds:

  • Fosc: the oscillation frequency, Hz;
  • Aosc: the generator's amplitude at the answer, which is the oscillation's own amplitude at the probe (volts in shunt, amperes of loop current in series);
  • Startup [freq]: the small-signal admittance looking into the probe, the curve the start-up search read.

Tuning. Inside a parametric sweep, Fosc against the swept variable is a tuning curve. A measurement gives Kvco, the slope of that curve, with deriv():

analysis SW1 type=parametric_sweep Var=Vtune Start=0 Stop=5 Step=0.25 Inner=OSC1
measure Kvco = deriv(OSC1.Fosc)

What moves the frequency. The oscillation sits a little away from the start-up frequency. As the swing grows, the active device spends part of each cycle cut off, and the capacitance and conductance it adds to the resonator change. A Colpitts, for example, starts where its transistor's low emitter impedance loads one capacitor, and settles toward the resonance of the tank with the transistor off. The harmonics pull the frequency a little too, so raise MaxHarm until Fosc stops moving.

Phase noise

PhaseNoise=yes computes the oscillation's phase noise from the circuit's own noise sources, so the effect of the resonator's Q, the device's flicker noise and the bias can be designed instead of assumed:

analysis OSC1 type=osc Fguess=140 Unit=MHz MaxHarm=9 PhaseNoise=yes PnStart=100 Hz PnStop=10 MHz PnNpts=61

The noise sources are the ones harmonic-balance noise uses: passive parts at their own temperature, each device's noise over its own large-signal waveform (shot, channel and flicker, as the model declares them), and a Mixer's NF. Every Port and Term is the oscillator's load, a resistance at 290 K.

Two descriptions, joined. Far from the carrier, the noise at the carrier's two sidebands comes straight from the conversion matrix about the oscillation, and splits into its phase part (PN) and its amplitude part (AN). Close to the carrier, nothing holds the oscillation's phase, so the noise moves its frequency instead. There the analysis uses how far the frequency moves for a small current at each noise source, which the oscillator's own solve already knows: white noise gives a 1/f² slope, flicker noise a 1/f³ slope. The two are joined at one tenth of the rate at which the oscillation's amplitude settles after a disturbance. Both hold there, and if they differ by more than 0.5 dB, the run warns.

Circuit Envelope (modulated signals)

Computes: the circuit's response to a modulated signal — LTE, NR, several carriers, a pulsed-CW train, or an imported I/Q waveform from a MOD_Source, designed in the Signal Designer — as a time series of harmonic-balance spectra, and from it the modulated measurements: ACLR / ACPR1 / ACPR2 per carrier, channel power, PAPR and CCDF, AM-AM and AM-PM (with the memory spread), gain, error (NMSE) against the input, drain efficiency from the DC supply currents, and for two widely spaced carriers the IM2 / IM3 / IM5 products. For a pulsed-CW source it measures inside the pulses instead: pulse droop, pulse-to-pulse spread, phase droop, rise and fall (see Pulsed CW: what is measured).

Method. Every envelope time step is one harmonic-balance solve with a time derivative added to each charge (Gear-2, backward Euler on the first step). The linear network is factored once per harmonic for the whole run, and each step costs one back-substitution per harmonic plus a small Newton solve at the nonlinear devices, reusing its factored Jacobian while it keeps converging fast. Each Newton update is damped by a line search, so a device driven hard at a signal peak, or one with trap or thermal states, does not throw the solve off. A step that still does not converge is retaken as up to 16 shorter steps, and the Messages pane says how many were.

Three things make long modulated runs practical:

  • Carriers far apart get their own fundamental. Two 20 MHz carriers 400 MHz apart are simulated with each carrier's narrow envelope on a two-tone mixing lattice, so the time step follows each carrier's bandwidth, not the 400 MHz spacing. A single envelope around the centre would need a step fine enough for the IM3 products 600 MHz out; measured, that is 64× the native rate to converge. Envelope = single runs it that way anyway: the whole multi-carrier signal as one time vector around Fc, with the step chosen so the band holds the carriers and their IM3. Use it as a cross-check. Measured on two NR20 carriers 400 MHz apart, at fine, against the lattice:

    • channel power and ACPR1 agree within 0.1 dB, and ACPR2 within 0.5 dB at a −58 dBc floor;
    • IM3L, IM2, f1+f2 and the second harmonics agree within 0.2 dB.

    Products far from Fc are not reliable in a single envelope. IM3U moves 0.3 dB and IM5 moves 1–4 dB when only the envelope's centre frequency moves. The lattice result does not move (to 0.01 dB), so the lattice is the reference. Both modes write the same IMD cube. The spectra (VSD and PSD) are continuous either way, and show every product of the baseband and fundamental zones.

  • An LO is a P1Tone, and gets its own fundamental. A P1Tone on one of the frequencies the run already computes (the signal's own frequency or one of its harmonics) drives that product. A P1Tone anywhere else, such as a receiver's LO 350 MHz from the signal, is a fundamental of its own, and the signal and the LO mix on the two-tone lattice. Two fundamentals are the most a run has, so a second LO must sit on a product of the other two. A P1Tone closer to a carrier than three bandwidths, but not on its frequency, is refused: its envelope would overlap the carrier's. With Envelope = single a P1Tone off the one envelope's harmonics drives nothing, and the run warns.

  • Parallel in time. The record is split into segments that run on all cores at once. Each segment starts a warm-up early from its own harmonic-balance point and discards it. The warm-up follows the circuit's slowest memory: five time constants of the slowest of a ComposedFET's trap and thermal states and the linear network's slowest pole (a bias feed, a large decoupling capacitor), and never less than 5 µs. When that memory is too long to warm up in parallel, the run is one segment and the Messages pane says which part remembers how long; Segments=N splits it anyway. Once the warm-up is past the circuit's memory, the stitched result matches one sequential run. Where the segments overlap, their disagreement relative to each recorded voltage's and IProbe current's own level is reported as the seam error (__EnvSeamError), and a run warns, naming the net or probe, when it is above 0.01 outside a device's internal states. In a parametric sweep the points also run at the same time, up to one per core, and share the cores with their segments.

  • The step follows an accuracy preset. standard (default) steps at 4× the signal's sample rate, where ACPR2 and the bias-node memory are converged on every measured case. draft (1×) is about 0.4 dB pessimistic on ACPR2 and badly off on bias-network ringing. fine (8×) is for reference runs. Whatever the preset, the step reaches every carrier's band edge, the highest frequency in its envelope, at least ten times a cycle, as the signal rate does for a carrier at the envelope's centre. A carrier off the centre, one of several in a signal or in a group, is stepped more finely when it needs it, and the Messages pane says so. Gear-2 damps a frequency f in the envelope by θ⁴/(4h), θ = 2πfh, and warps it slightly; through a filter's delay the damping tilts the gain across a carrier off the centre, which reads as EVM. At draft, a 5 MHz NR carrier 4.5 MHz off the centre was stepped at 0.7 rad at its band edge and read 1.1 % EVM through a 60 MHz bandpass, where the same carrier centred reads 0.38 %; it now reads 0.39 %. The rule bounds the step, not the error, which still grows with the offset and with the filter's delay: through the same bandpass at draft a carrier 3.5 MHz off reads about 0.69 % (the rule does not act there) and one 22 MHz off about 1.5 %; at standard, 15 MHz off reads 0.60 %, where fine reads 0.36 %. For EVM through a filter, use standard or fine, or put the source's Fc on the carrier you measure.

Frequency-dependent blocks. Resistors, capacitors and inductors enter every step exactly. Other blocks are handled as follows.

  • Lines (TLIN, and the microstrip, stripline and coplanar lines) are stepped as a delay: each end sees the wave that left the other end one line delay earlier. A lossless line is exact at any length, across the signal band and in the baseband envelope (the bias path). A lossy line's loss is taken to first order around each harmonic and left out of the baseband envelope, where the line stays exact at DC. A line is usually shorter than one step, so the delayed wave is interpolated between steps: at standard this is off by up to about 1.5 % at the edge of the signal band, at fine about 0.2 %. The Messages pane gives each line's delay and how far it is from the line across the band, and a warning names any line far enough off to move ACPR by about 0.1 dB.
  • S-parameter blocks (SnP), the System Filter and Duplexer, an Impedance N-Port with a Z(freq), and any other part whose response bends across a harmonic's band by more than the fit's error tolerance (a Klopfenstein taper, for one) are fitted with a rational model and simulated exactly across the signal band, at DC and at every harmonic. The Messages pane reports each fit's order and error, and, for an SnP, how far the fit is from the file's interpolated values at DC and at any harmonic outside the file's frequency range. An SnP, Filter or Duplexer whose fit misses its error tolerance, or is not passive, warns. Any other part whose fit misses or is not passive is not fitted: it is approximated to first order around each harmonic (its value and slope there) and, where its DC value is closer to it than that slope, held at its DC value across the baseband envelope; a warning names it.
  • Bond wires (wBond) are a resistance and an inductance that change only slowly with frequency (the skin effect), plus fixed capacitances. Their value and slope at each harmonic are within about 10⁻⁷ of their response across the signal band, and within 0.1 % across the baseband envelope.
  • Coupled lines (CLIN and the coupled microstrip and broadside pairs) are approximated to first order around each harmonic (their value and slope there). In the baseband envelope each strip is held at what it is at DC, a short from end to end. A warning names any whose error across the band is large enough to move ACPR by about 0.1 dB.

What a run records

Name a net to see its voltage. Put an IProbe where you want power.

  • Every labeled net records its voltage. A schematic labels a net by naming it, or with a VProbe on it; a netlist labels nets on its labelednets line. A netlist that labels nothing records its MOD_Source's node.
  • Every IProbe records the power that flows through it, from its first node to its second. It is exact for any load, reactive ones included.

No impedance is assumed anywhere: a voltage is reported as a voltage, and a power is measured where a current is known. Without an IProbe an envelope run reports voltages only; place one in series with a load to measure its power. A chain of 50 Ω system blocks needs one IProbe in front of its load.

The envelope card shows what the schematic will record ("Records 4 labeled nets and 3 IProbes"); hover over it for the names. Every recorded net keeps its envelopes, so a design that labels 40 nets, swept over five drive levels, keeps about 1 GB of waveforms. Set SaveWaveforms to no to keep only the measurements.

Power and efficiency

Two quantities never depend on where the output is, and are scalars:

Scalar How it is calculated
Pin_avg_dBm The available power of the drive: the mean of |Vdrive(t)|² / (8 · Re Zsource), where Vdrive is the source's open-circuit envelope. This is not the power delivered into the input. Every MOD_Source counts.
Pdc_W The sum over every DC source with a non-zero Vdc of −Vdc · ⟨Idc(t)⟩, where Idc(t) is that source's DC branch current at each envelope step. The current follows the modulation, so this is the true average supply power. Gate and auxiliary supplies count; a resistive bias network's own dissipation lowers DE_pct.

At every IProbe:

Cube How it is calculated
Pavg_dBm The fundamental-band power through the probe. Everything inside the carrier's envelope counts, including the adjacent-channel and distortion power. Power at 2f₀, 3f₀ … does not.
Gt_dB Pavg / Pin_avg: transducer gain from the available input power.
DE_pct 100 · Pavg / Pdc: average drain efficiency in the fundamental band. Right for one amplifier; blank when no DC power is drawn.

Each is a time average over the measured record (after the warm-up). With two fundamentals (carriers on the mixing lattice), Pdc_W and DE_pct are not produced; the supply probe's (0,0) product gives the DC power.

Where to place IProbes

Put one in series with each place you want a power:

  • the load, for output power,
  • the input, between the source and the amplifier's input match, for delivered input power,
  • each supply, between the DC source and the circuit, for DC power.

With the probe's first node on the source side, each of these powers is positive in a working amplifier. The probe node's voltage is what AM-PM, NMSE and the delay are read from, so the output probe belongs at the load itself, not ahead of an output match.

One amplifier. With IProbe:Iin at the input, IProbe:Iout at the load and IProbe:IDC at the drain supply:

Delivered input power Pavg_dBm at Iin

Two amplifiers, A and B, each with its own load and supply: place IoutA, IDCA, IoutB and IDCB. Each amplifier's drain efficiency is its own Pprobe_fund_W at Iout over its own Pprobe_W at IDC, harmonic 0, and the total DC power is the sum of the two.

AM-AM, AM-PM and memory

With one fundamental and a modulated drive, every IProbe gets AM-AM and AM-PM against the instantaneous available drive power, |Vdrive(t)|² / (8 · Re Zsource), on a fixed grid of 0.5 dB bins (axis Pin_inst, −40 to 60 dBm). A bin needs 16 samples; one with fewer is blank.

  • The probe node's fundamental envelope is aligned to the drive (the delay Delay_s reports).
  • AM-AM is the delivered power ½·Re(V₁·I₁*) over the available drive, sample by sample, averaged in each bin. It is exact for any load.
  • AM-PM is the phase of the probe node's voltage relative to the drive, less the mean gain phase.
  • AMAM_min / AMAM_max and AMPM_min / AMPM_max are the extremes of the samples in each bin. Memory widens them: the same drive level meets a different trap, thermal or bias state. So does the bin itself: in compression the gain changes across 0.5 dB of drive, by about 0.5 dB on a device whose gain falls 1 dB per dB.

The memory cloud is every one of those samples on one time axis, so the spread can be seen point by point rather than as two edges:

Cube Axes Meaning
Pin_inst_t_dBm time the available drive power at each sample
Gt_t_dB time, branch the gain at each sample: the values AM-AM bins
PM_t_deg time, branch the phase at each sample: the values AM-PM bins

They keep at most 20,000 samples per probe (every m-th, m set by Stop and the step alone, so a sweep stacks), and they are kept with SaveWaveforms no. Plot the cloud with a versus trace drawn with markers and no line:

Pulsed CW: what is measured

A pulsed-CW source turns the run into a pulse measurement. Harmonic balance cannot see what happens inside a pulse: it solves one steady state, so the device sits at one temperature and one trap state. An envelope run steps a ComposedFET's thermal and trap states, and the bias network's storage, as real memory over time, so the output falls across the top of each pulse as the channel heats and the drain supply sags.

The run starts from harmonic balance at the first sample of the record, which for a pulse is the off-time, so the first pulse starts from the quiescent point. A record that started on the pulse top would start with the device already at its CW temperature, and the first pulse would show no droop at all; that is why a pulse's Delay must be greater than zero.

At every IProbe, from the power delivered through it, it adds:

Cube Axes Meaning
Pout_t_dBm branch, time delivered power at every step, floored at −100 dBm in the off-time, so it plots with no arithmetic
PulseTop_dBm branch, pulse the power at the droop window's start
PulseDroop_dB branch, pulse the power at the window's start minus at its end: positive is drooping
PulsePhaseDroop_deg branch, pulse the probe node's phase (relative to the input) at the window's start minus at its end
PulseRise_s, PulseFall_s branch, pulse the 10–90 % times of the amplitude (the square root of the delivered power)
PulseToPulse_dB, PulseToPulse_deg branch the peak-to-peak spread over the pulses of PulseTop_dBm, and of the phase at the window's start; the first pulse counts

The droop window is the flat top from MeasureStart to MeasureEnd (10–90 % of the top by default; set them on the carrier). A pulse only partly inside Stop is not measured, and the Messages pane says how many were and over which window. ACLR, CCDF, PAPR, AM-AM / AM-PM and NMSE are not reported for a pulse: they are defined for a modulated signal, and on a pulse train they print numbers that look like results. Pin_avg_dBm, Pavg_dBm, Gt_dB, Pdc_W and DE_pct are averages over the whole run, off-time included, with no settling skipped (the train starts at quiescent and has nothing to settle), so they depend on how many pulses Stop holds. Pdc_W is what the DC sources deliver, so a drain fed from a storage capacitor draws most of each pulse's energy from the capacitor and repays the supply after the pulse: end Stop once the last pulse's recharge is over (about five of the supply resistance × storage capacitance time constants after its fall), or DE_pct reads high — above 100 % on a run that stops at the end of a pulse.

Before the run starts, the Messages pane states how many pulses Stop holds and the circuit's slowest memory — a thermal pole, a trap's emission, the bias network — against it: a run shorter than about five of those time constants shows the first pulse from cold, not a settled train. A drain-lag trap that captures at the pulse peak in nanoseconds and emits over a millisecond lowers the quiescent current after the first pulse, so pulse 1 differs from pulse 2: that is current collapse, the effect a pulse-to-pulse figure exists to report. A thermal train settles only after several of its slowest time constants, which on a package pole means milliseconds of record at a step of a few nanoseconds.

From the command line: circuitrf env design.cnl [--stop 0.5e-3] [--accuracy standard] [-o out.npy]. It prints each IProbe's power, gain, efficiency, NMSE and PAPR, and the ACLR and signal quality (EVM, FSK error or the de-chirped error) at every probe that carries RF. For a pulsed-CW source it prints each pulse's top power, droop, phase droop, rise and fall at those probes, then the pulse-to-pulse spread.

Noise and phase noise

Without these settings every signal in an envelope run is noiseless: the spectrum has no floor and an LO is a perfect tone. Two settings add what a receiver is limited by.

Thermal noise (Noise=yes). Every part the S-parameter noise analysis makes noisy adds noise to the run: resistors and other passive parts at their own temperature, S-parameter blocks with noise data, system amplifiers with an NF, and a Mixer's NF as a source at its RF port, as in harmonic balance. At every step, each harmonic band gets a random current on the part's nodes, with the part's noise at the centre of that band. The noise is taken as flat across one band, so a part whose noise changes noticeably across the signal bandwidth is evaluated at the band centre. The PSD then shows the noise floor, and ACLR includes it.

  • Sources and ports are noiseless unless you say otherwise. To put a source's own noise in the run, tick Noisy in the source's parameters (Noise=yes on a P1Tone, PnTone or MOD_Source): its impedance then adds thermal noise at the source's temperature, Temp if set, else the ambient. Set Temp=16.85 (290 K) for the input noise a noise figure is referred to. A P1Tone or PnTone with per-harmonic impedances uses each band's own. The S-parameter, harmonic-balance and loadpull noise analyses keep the source noiseless, because their noise figure already counts the source at 290 K.
  • A transistor's or diode's own noise is not added: it changes with the device's state inside a step. The run names such devices in one warning, and they run noiseless.

Phase noise (PhaseNoise= on a source). P1Tone, PnTone, the tone sources and MOD_Source take a phase-noise mask, single-sided L(f) in dBc/Hz at offsets from the carrier:

P1Tone:LO  lo 0  Pavl=7 dBm  Freq=2 GHz  PhaseNoise="1kHz:-80, 10kHz:-100, 100kHz:-120, 1MHz:-140"
  • Between points the mask is a straight line in dB against log-frequency. Below the first point, the first segment's slope continues. Beyond the last point it is PhaseNoiseFloor (dBc/Hz), or the last point's level when that is not set.
  • The run turns the source's phase by a random φ(t) whose spectrum is the mask: by φ at its fundamental and k·φ at its k-th harmonic. An LO with a mask mixes a blocker's energy into the wanted channel, which is reciprocal mixing.
  • An envelope represents offsets up to half its sample rate. Mask points beyond that cannot be simulated, and the run says so once, naming the first such offset.
  • Harmonic balance, loadpull and S-parameters run at steady state and ignore the mask. check reports it as information on those analyses.

Seed. The same Seed gives the same noise on every run and on every computer, and a run split into parallel segments draws the same noise as one run. Every point of a parametric sweep uses the same seed, so the differences between points come from the circuit; SeedPerPoint=yes gives each point its own.

CSS: CSSerr_pct, the square root of the energy outside each symbol's own FFT bin over the energy in it, after de-chirping, in percent. FSK and CSS have constant envelopes, so a constellation would be a circle; these say more.

  • How it is measured. The probe's envelope is brought back to the carrier's own sample rate and its delay is fitted against the signal the source played. OFDM is then cut into symbols, each transformed after its cyclic prefix, with one complex gain fitted against the ideal points. DBPSK, FSK and CSS are first filtered to their channel, and so is the reference they are compared with, so the measurement bandwidth is the channel's. There is no per-subcarrier equaliser, so the in-band ripple of a filter counts as error, as it does in NMSE.
  • Through a matched pad the error is the source signal's own: the Signal Designer's EVM for LTE and NR (from clipping and shaping), and zero for DBPSK, FSK and CSS.
  • The step. EVM reads the circuit as the run steps it, and through a filter draft reads more than standard: a 5 MHz NR carrier through a 60 MHz bandpass reads 0.38 % at draft and 0.34 % at standard when it is at the envelope's centre. Off the centre the difference grows with the offset and with the filter's delay (the step), so measure EVM through a filter at standard or fine, or with the source's Fc on the carrier.
  • The record. A run of whole records (Stop a multiple of the source's record, with a warm-up) measures every symbol. A shorter run measures the symbols, bits or chirps it holds whole, apart from a few samples at each end. A run shorter than one has no result, and the run says so.
  • After a mixer. A carrier is read where it arrives. At a probe after a mixer, the carrier sits at the IF, not at its own frequency, and it is measured there: of the products that carry it through an LO's fundamental (a P1Tone's, or a CW carrier's), the one with the most power at that probe. Its EVM, and the probe's Pavg_dBm, Gt_dB, ACLR and PAPR_dB, are all read at that product. An IF below the LO (f_LO − f_RF) holds the carrier mirrored, and it is read mirrored back, its lower and upper adjacent channels included.
  • Other carriers. CW, a pulse and an imported I/Q file carry no symbols: their cells are blank, the run names them once, and NMSE remains their measure. Each set of cubes is written only when a carrier of its kind is in the run, and a run with none writes none of them.

Distortion and noise (EvmSplit=yes). With Noise=yes or a PhaseNoise mask, EvmSplit runs the same record a second time with every noise source and mask off. Each error then splits into its distortion and its noise, and the in-channel SNR is measured:

Cube Meaning
EVMdist_pct, FSKerrDist_pct, CSSerrDist_pct the error without noise: the distortion
EVMnoise_pct, FSKerrNoise_pct, CSSerrNoise_pct √(error² − distortion²): the noise's part
SNR_dB the in-channel signal over the in-channel noise, the noise being the difference between the two runs

A source's PhaseNoise mask is off in the second run too, so phase noise is in the noise part and lowers SNR_dB as thermal noise does. An LO whose mask integrates to −38.5 dBc over the channel holds the noise part near 1 % however strong the signal; the distortion part is the error without it.

For LTE, NR, DBPSK and CSS the noise's part is 1/√SNR. FSK reads its frequency, and noise moves the frequency more than the amplitude, so its error is larger than that. The split is what decides whether to fix linearity or noise. Over a short record the noisy run can read slightly less than the noiseless one; the noise part is then 0 and the run says so. Without EvmSplit, SNR_dB is not written: the spectrum's floor alone would mix distortion into it. explain states the second run, and check says when there is nothing to split.

Loadpull / Sourcepull

Computes: figures of merit (Pout, gain, efficiency, PAE) as the load (or source) reflection coefficient is swept over a grid on the Smith chart — the classic loadpull experiment — ready to draw as contours. Harmonic terminations can be swept too (harmonic loadpull).

Method: a programmable Tuner presents each grid termination; a harmonic-balance solve runs per point (with previous-point continuation for convergence); the FOMs are collected into the DataSet over the Γ-grid. The Data Display fits a surface and extracts contour lines; measured loadpull files (.spl/.lpcwave) plot identically.

The grid points run in parallel, and the result is the same, bit for bit, as one point after another: the numbers, the warnings and the exported files. Each point starts from the nearest earlier point's first drive level, so the points wait only for each other's first solve.

  • How many at once. By default one per core, and never more than one per two points. Workers=1 runs them one at a time and Workers=4 uses at most four cores, which is polite on a shared machine. In the analysis dialog this is Grid points at once, under Advanced; 0 is the default. A pursuit takes the same key for its follow-on loadpull.
  • When they run one at a time anyway. A design with a compiled (.osdi) or vendor-kit device: the device lives in one worker process, which answers one call at a time. A design whose SDD reads a control current (C[n], _cn). And inside an optimizer, yield, corner or DOE run that already simulates its points side by side, so that it does not start one worker per core for each of them.
  • The progress lines printed while it runs come in grid order. A few other lines a solve may print as it goes, such as a singular-Jacobian notice or an expression's domain clamp, can appear out of that order; the results, warnings and notes do not depend on it.

Noise figure in loadpull and sourcepull

Noise=yes adds the noise figure to a loadpull, beside Pout, gain and PAE. Swept on the source side (Sweep=Source), its contours with gain and PAE on the same chart show the trade an input match is chosen on.

analysis LP1 type=loadpull Tone=2.4 ToneUnit=GHz LoadTuner=TL SourceTuner=TS Grid="source.gam" Sweep=Source Noise=yes

At every drive level of every grid point that converges, circuitRF reads the harmonic-balance noise on that drive level's own large-signal waveform. NF [gridPoint, pinStep] (dB, SSB) is written with the other results, the contour plot offers it as a metric, and a .spl export carries it as one more column. A .spl reader that predates the NF column skips it and reads the rest.

Why not at the tone itself. Under drive, noise near the carrier is low-frequency noise that the drive carries up, so how much of it there is depends on how close you look. 1 kHz off the tone, flicker noise and a high-impedance bias network can add several dB that a signal 10 MHz away never sees. By default the noise is read 1 % above the tone, the noise a small signal beside the drive sees. For a receive band, set NoiseFreq to it. A NoiseFreq exactly at the tone, or at any half-multiple of it, is read 1e-6 of the tone above, and the run says so.

The tuners are noiseless, as ports are. The input termination is at T0 = 290 K. At the low drive levels of the ladder, NF equals the S-parameter noise analysis on the same terminations. A small-signal sourcepull's NF contours are the circles ns_circle draws.

It costs about one Newton iteration per drive level. On a pursuit, the same keys add NF to its follow-on loadpull. The search itself does not read noise.

The termination grid file (.gam)

The grid of terminations a loadpull visits lives in a small, human-readable text file with a .gam extension — one termination per line. It is kept out of the netlist on purpose: a grid is data (often hundreds of points), and a real tuner system works the same way, reading a "pattern file" of impedance states to visit. A loadpull analysis points its grid at a .gam file; a Loadpull Pursuit can write one for you (its recommended terminations).

Each point is either an impedance (Ω) or a reflection coefficient Γ, in one of three column layouts. An optional header line (starting #) declares the form, the reference impedance, and the layout; if you omit it, circuitRF assumes impedance and infers the layout from the first data line. The parser is deliberately forgiving — blank lines and ; or # comment lines are skipped.

Loadpull Pursuit

Computes: the load (or source) terminations that optimize a figure of merit — found automatically by a query-minimizing search rather than by gridding the whole Smith chart. It reports two optima at constant compression — MXP (maximum output power) and MXE (maximum efficiency) — recommends a conjugate-match source impedance for each, and can run a focused high-fidelity loadpull around them, all from one analysis.

Why use it: it automates the real-world PA loadpull procedure — find the optimum, then loadpull a focused grid around it with a sensible source match — into a single unattended run. Point it at a DUT whose optimal terminations are unknown, run, and come back to a complete loadpull dataset already concentrated at the right terminations. It is repeatable (great for characterizing many devices with one netlist), avoids wasted query points far from the optima, and avoids non-convergent terminations.

It complements, it doesn't replace, a standard loadpull. Pursuit finds the useful terminations and the region of interest; a standard loadpull then shows the performance tradeoffs across that region. Pursuit shares all Loadpull settings except the Γ grid (it generates terminations rather than reading them), plus the keys below.

How a pursuit run works

  1. Search — a steepest-ascent search in the VSWR plane finds MXP, then MXE (seeded from MXP, so the second search is cheap). Each "query" is one drive-to-compression harmonic-balance run; queries are cached so MXE reuses MXP's data.
  2. Recommend — builds a focused-around-the-optima set of recommended terminations (dense near MXP/MXE, sparse further out) and a conjugate-match source impedance for each optimum.
  3. Loadpull — optionally runs a standard loadpull over those recommended terminations, using a recommended source match, producing the high-fidelity contour data.

Search method

  • SteepestAscent (default) — fits the gradient and ascends along a fixed direction, shrinking the step on rejection; a final polynomial refinement pins the optimum. Fast and adequate for the near-1-D real-axis optima typical of PA loadpull.

  • IteratedQuadratic — a trust-region search that re-fits the local curvature at every step, so it follows a curved 2-D ridge instead of committing to one direction. Reuses the same query cache, so its query count stays comparable.

Multi-Tone Harmonic Balance (2 to 6 tones)

Computes: the steady-state spectrum of a nonlinear circuit driven by several carriers at once — every harmonic and every intermodulation (mixing) product. Two tones is the standard test for linearity: third-order intermodulation (IM3), intercept points, spectral regrowth, and the asymmetry between the lower and upper IM products. Three or more tones is the natural setting for mixers (RF, LO and their products) and for multi-carrier work. Two tones is enough for an ideal mixer on its own — one for the RF, one for the LO — and the converted power reads off the IF port at the mixing product you want.

Multi-tone is not a separate analysis — it is Harmonic Balance with more than one tone. Everything from the single-tone chapter (the linear/nonlinear split, the Newton solve, drive continuation, all the convergence knobs) still applies. This chapter covers only what is different.

Up to six tones are supported. Everything below is written for two because that is the common case, but it holds at any tone count: read "(k₁, k₂)" as the full list "(k₁, …, kT)". The one thing that changes with tone count is how far you can push Max mix order — see How many tones, at what order.

Setting it up

In the Harmonic Balance analysis editor, switch the Single / Multi-tone toggle to Multi-tone. Three things change:

  • The single Tone (f₀) field is replaced by a tone list, starting with Tone 1 and Tone 2. + Tone adds another (up to six); the × on a row removes it, down to a minimum of two.

  • Max harmonics is replaced by Max mix order — the truncation that bounds the two-dimensional mixing spectrum (see below).

  • Every tone accepts an expression, so a common pattern is Tone&nbsp;1 = RFfreq − Spacing/2, Tone&nbsp;2 = RFfreq + Spacing/2 with RFfreq and Spacing as variables you can sweep.

  • Beside Max mix order the editor shows how many mixing products the current tone count and order retain, and warns when that is over the limit — so you can see the cost of an order before you run it.

The analysis owns the tones, not the source

The fundamentals come from the HB analysis (the tone list, written as NumFreqs=N Tone[1]=… Tone[N]=… in the netlist), not from the source. A PnTone (or V_nTone) just supplies power at those frequencies. Every source tone must land exactly on the analysis grid — an off-grid drive frequency is rejected at setup with a clear message rather than producing silent garbage.

The mixing grid and mixing order

With two fundamentals, a spectral line is no longer indexed by a single harmonic number. Each line is a pair of integers (k₁, k₂) sitting at the physical frequency

f = k₁·f₁ + k₂·f₂

and its mixing order is m = |k₁| + |k₂|. The carriers are (1, 0) and (0, 1) at order 1; a third-order intermod such as 2f₁−f₂ is (2, −1) at order 3. Solving for every possible (k₁, k₂) is infinite, so circuitRF keeps a finite set bounded by Max mix order:

retain (k₁, k₂)  ⟺  |k₁| + |k₂| ≤ MaxMixOrder

That inequality is a diamond (a rotated square) in the (k₁, k₂) plane — hence "diamond truncation." A diamond rather than a full rectangular box because the high–high corner products (e.g. 5f₁+5f₂) carry no meaningful energy in a real intermod test, while every low-order product that does matter is inside the diamond. MaxMixOrder is the two-tone analog of single-tone MaxHarm: order 5 retains products down to IM5. The number of products grows quadratically with the order (≈ 2·order² lines), so a two-tone run is much larger than a single-tone run at the same order — order 5 yields dozens of mixing products versus a handful of harmonics.

With T tones the same rule reads retain (k₁,&nbsp;…,&nbsp;k<sub>T</sub>) ⟺ |k₁| + … + |k<sub>T</sub>| ≤ MaxMixOrder, and the diamond becomes a T-dimensional one. There is still a single order knob — no per-tone limits.

Reading the spectrum in the Data Display

A multi-tone result plotted as a spectrum uses a mixIndex axis: each stem is a mixing product, drawn at its physical frequency, labelled with its (k₁, k₂) tag — or (k₁, …, kT) at more tones. Measuring intermodulation (IMD2 / IMD3)

A measurement reads a mixing product out of the result by its (k₁, k₂) tag — at three tones the tag simply has three entries, e.g. HB1.V("Vout",&nbsp;"(1,1,-1)") for the triple-beat product f₁+f₂−f₃. The classic figures of merit are the intermodulation ratios in dBc — an IM product relative to its carrier:

IMD2 = dB( HB1.V("Vout", "(1,-1)") ) - dB( HB1.V("Vout", "(1,0)") )   dB
IMD3 = dB( HB1.V("Vout", "(2,-1)") ) - dB( HB1.V("Vout", "(1,0)") )   dB

Replace HB1 with your analysis name and "Vout" with your output node. IMD2 uses the baseband product (1,−1) = f₁−f₂; IMD3 uses the lower third-order product (2,−1) = 2f₁−f₂ (use (−1,2), referenced to the (0,1) carrier, for the upper IM3). To get the curve vs Pin, sweep the drive power (a parametric sweep over the variable your source Pavl references) — the same expression then returns IMD vs Pin instead of a single value.

Two ways to address a product — use the accessor for IMD

The accessor HB1.V("Vout", "(1,-1)") names the node and the product tag and keeps any swept axes automatically, so the same expression works whether or not you have added a Pin sweep — one value without it, a curve over Pin with it. The bracket form HB1.V[:, "Vout", "(1,-1)"] is positional: one token per cube axis (a leading : to keep the Pin sweep), so it must be re-edited when a sweep is added or removed. Prefer the accessor for IMD metrics you keep; reach for the bracket when copy-pasting from a trace card. See Referencing analysis results.

Source impedance and harmonic terminations

With dozens of products spread across the spectrum, each one needs a source termination. circuitRF assigns it by frequency, not by mixing order. It defines a band-center fundamental — for two tones the average f_c = (f₁ + f₂) / 2 — and places each product in the harmonic band nearest its frequency:

band n = round( |f| / f_c ) → Z[n] if declared, else the shared Z

So band 0 (|f| < f_c/2) is the baseband and uses Z[0]; band 1 (around f_c) holds both carriers and all the close-in IM3/IM5 products and uses Z[1] (or the default Z); band 2 (around 2·f_c) holds the second-harmonic products and uses Z[2]; and so on. The decision is purely the product's frequency: as a high-order product such as (3, −1) drifts down past the band-1/band-2 boundary it switches from Z[2] to Z[1] automatically — no order threshold is hard-coded. This is the same harmonic-termination model as P1Tone, and a PnTone presents its shared Z and per-band Z[k] to the whole multi-tone spectrum this way.

Why baseband termination matters

In single-tone HB the baseband is decoupled. In two-tone it is not — the IM2 product (1, −1) at f₁−f₂ lands at baseband, and its termination Z[0] feeds back into the carriers and the IM3 levels. Studying source/load baseband-termination effects is a primary reason to run two-tone, so set Z[0] deliberately.

What's different from single-tone

  • IM3 asymmetry. The lower IM3 (2f₁−f₂) and upper IM3 (2f₂−f₁) generally have different levels in dBc, not just a phase difference — a direct consequence of the device's nonlinear I–V/Q–V behaviour and its loading. The full diamond resolves both, so the asymmetry is a real output of the run, not an artifact.

  • A fully coupled solve. The two-tone result is not two single-tone solutions superimposed. The carriers, harmonics, IM products, and baseband are all unknowns in one Newton solve with a single conversion-matrix Jacobian — so the IM levels capture the true bidirectional coupling between the carriers and the baseband.

  • A bigger spectrum, a bigger cost. Because the product count grows quadratically with MaxMixOrder, raise it only as far as you need: 5 captures IM5 (usually enough); higher captures IM7+ at noticeably more cost.

  • Commensurate tones. f₁ and f₂ must be commensurate (a rational ratio) so every mixing product lands exactly on the grid and the waveform is exactly periodic — closely-spaced tones like 1.99 / 2.01 GHz satisfy this naturally.

TRL Calibration

A TRL calibration finds the two fixtures between your instrument and the part you measured (the launches, probes or connectors on each side), from three standards measured through those same fixtures. It writes them as two .s2p files, which two Deembed blocks then take off any other measurement made through the same fixtures. It solves no circuit.

Place three SnP blocks on a test bench, one for each measured standard, and add an analysis of type TRL Calibration:

  • Thru: the two fixtures connected directly. The reference plane is the middle of the Thru.
  • Reflect: the same high-reflection standard on both ports, port 1's in S11 and port 2's in S22. You only say whether it is an open or a short; its exact value is solved for, and reported.
  • Line: a longer piece of line between the fixtures. Its impedance becomes the reference impedance of everything you de-embed, so make it the impedance you want to read results in.
  • Fixture A and Fixture B: where the two files are written. They are resolved exactly as an SnP's File is, so the same text goes into the Deembed's File. Left empty, they are <analysis name>_A.s2p and _B.s2p.

Fixture A's port 1 is the instrument side and port 2 the part's. Fixture B's port 1 is the part's side and port 2 the instrument's. So the chain reads left to right as the measurement did: Port 1 — Deembed(A) — measured part — Deembed(B) — Port 2, with no flipping.

The usable band. TRL tells the two fixtures apart by how much longer the Line is than the Thru. Where that difference is near 0° or 180°, the two standards are nearly the same and the answer is unreliable. A run warns about every point outside 20° to 160° (modulo 180°), naming the frequencies. One line covers 8:1. A line that is 90° longer at the middle of your band, f_mid = (f_low + f_high) / 2, is a good choice; cover a wider band with a second line and a second calibration.

The analysis's result holds SA and SB (the two fixtures, as S cubes), LineDeg (the Line's extra length over the Thru, in degrees), LineLossDb, Gamma (the solved reflect) and Usable (1 inside the band, 0 outside), to plot like any other result. A measurement reads a fixture's entry by port number, as it reads S: abs(CAL.SA(2, 1)).

SnP:THRU     t1 t2 File="measurements/thru.s2p"    NumPorts=2
SnP:REFLECT  r1 r2 File="measurements/reflect.s2p" NumPorts=2
SnP:LINE     l1 l2 File="measurements/line.s2p"    NumPorts=2
analysis CAL type=trl thru=THRU reflect=REFLECT line=LINE reflectType=short fixtureA="fixtures/A.s2p" fixtureB="fixtures/B.s2p"

From the command line, circuitrf trl bench.csch runs it (trl).

A Deembed whose fixture file a TRL analysis in the workspace writes, run before that analysis, stops naming the bench to run first. It writes the Touchstone and the diagnostics .npy where the Simulate button writes them, so a schematic's SnP reference survives a headless re-run. Full detail, with a worked example.

Measurements, and why headless is the regression harness

The measure lines on the TestBench are evaluated exactly as the GUI evaluates them, and a measurement that fails to evaluate is reported on stderr and the run continues. A .cnl that works headless works when opened, which is what makes the command line usable as a regression harness: the two paths share the elaborator, the engines and the measurement evaluator.
